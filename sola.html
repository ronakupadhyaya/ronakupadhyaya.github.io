SOLA AND THE INVISIBLE ASYMPTOTE OF AUTOMATION
Why making automation effortless may expand the surface area of software

Before starting Sola, Jessica Wu worked in finance, where she encountered substantial manual work around an old brokerage system and found that even simple automations were difficult to build with existing RPA tools. Neil Deshmukh had a similar experience while building hospital systems at Massachusetts General Hospital, where the tooling was old, brittle, and difficult to implement. Wu later described the broader enterprise environment they encountered as one in which operations teams move across spreadsheets, internal portals, external systems, files, and other tools that often do not connect cleanly.1

This was precisely the class of problem robotic process automation was supposed to solve. RPA had an intuitively compelling thesis, namely, that the people closest to repetitive work understand it best, so they should be able to automate it themselves. However, in practice, much of the last generation of RPA moved in the opposite direction. Enterprises built centralized automation teams, hired consultants, and trained specialists to construct and maintain workflows that could be brittle when the surrounding software or inputs changed.2

Sola starts from a much more natural interface. Instead of asking someone to describe a process in the language of automation, it lets them begin by doing the work. A user records herself performing a task, and Sola uses language models and computer vision to turn those actions into a functional workflow that business users can understand, modify, maintain, and deploy.3

There is something rather powerful about recording as an authoring interface. The operator does not need to first learn how an automation platform represents loops, variables, branches, or interface elements before she can begin. She already knows how to perform the job, and recording allows that existing knowledge to become the starting point for software. This makes the original citizen-developer idea considerably more literal, as expertise in the business process itself becomes enough to begin automating it.

Together, these ideas explain why I find Sola more interesting than simply a better version of RPA. The immediate opportunity is to make a large body of existing automation easier to create and maintain, but the broader opportunity appears if Sola becomes the place where companies teach software how work gets done, assemble those capabilities into processes, and deploy them across the organization.

THE ECONOMICS OF AUTOMATION

Ally Logistics provides a useful example. The company has deployed Sola across billing, accounts receivable and payable, carrier compliance, account operations, and pricing, including workflows for proof-of-delivery uploads, load close-outs, and automated spot bidding.5 These are different parts of the business, but they share a common characteristic: employees would otherwise have to perform repetitive operational work across the systems they already use.

Cash application is one of them. Freight produces a large volume of transactions that have to be matched to specific shipment IDs to keep accounts receivable accurate and up to date. Before automating the process with Sola, Ally said the work took roughly 5 to 10 hours each week. In a public demonstration, an employee recorded himself processing several payments, after which Sola generated a workflow for review. He then configured an email trigger, extracted invoice numbers from an incoming PDF, replaced values from the recording with variables, reviewed the generated steps, and ran a test before using the workflow repeatedly.4

The magnitude of the time savings is useful, but the interaction model is more important. The employee begins with an instance of real work rather than a blank automation canvas. The distance between knowing how to perform the process and being able to encode it in software becomes dramatically shorter.

This makes automation easier to distribute inside an organization as well. Central automation teams necessarily have finite capacity, while the employees performing operational work encounter potential automation opportunities continuously. If the marginal creator of an automation can be the person already doing the job, discovery and creation can begin to happen at the edge of the organization rather than exclusively through a centralized queue.

Ally’s subsequent adoption suggests what happens when that threshold moves. Within two months, the company reported 45 active automations.4 Five months after going live, it reported 115 active workflows and 52,481 executions in the preceding 30 days across billing, accounts receivable and payable, carrier compliance, operations, and pricing.5 Once the organization had a sufficiently easy way to turn repetitive work into software, it kept discovering more work worth expressing through the same system.

THE INVISIBLE ASYMPTOTE

Eugene Wei used the phrase invisible asymptote to describe the hidden ceiling that eventually limits a product’s growth if it continues along its existing path. At Amazon, one of the earliest and most important asymptotes turned out to be shipping fees. Customers liked the underlying service, but their reluctance to pay for shipping constrained how frequently they were willing to use it. Identifying that ceiling gave Amazon a concrete problem around which years of product work could be organized.6

Process automation has a similar constraint, since there is no shortage of repetitive work that could theoretically be automated. The limitation is that every additional process carries some cost to understand, translate into software, test, and maintain. At some point, the value of automating the next process is simply smaller than the effort required to automate it.

That is the invisible asymptote Sola is pushing against.

The useful thought experiment is therefore to imagine what happens as that constraint approaches zero. What would automation look like if translating human work into reliable software became nearly effortless?

In the limit, every repetitive digital process that can reasonably be delegated to software is addressable. A user demonstrates the work once and receives a useful first draft immediately. The system understands enough of what was demonstrated to require very little additional configuration, while making the remaining uncertainty easy to resolve. The time between showing Sola a process and trusting the resulting automation approaches zero.

Once deployed, ordinary variation creates almost no ordinary maintenance. A field moves, a document arrives in a slightly different form, or a page loads more slowly, and the workflow continues. Humans become involved when the business itself is ambiguous, when a policy changes, or when judgment genuinely adds value rather than whenever the environment deviates from the original example.

The asymptote is useful because it makes the product direction clearer. If the constraint is the effort required to turn the next process into reliable software, the question becomes straightforward: what would make that next process cheaper to create, easier to trust, and simpler to compose with everything that already exists?

FROM ACTIONS TO UNDERSTANDING

The first opportunity follows directly from the way Sola begins. Recording is powerful precisely because it preserves the operator’s natural relationship with the work, so the interesting product question is how much more context can be recovered without recreating the configuration burden that recording was designed to remove.

One possibility is to give the demonstration a small amount of semantic context before it begins. The operator might describe the desired outcome in ordinary language. For instance, apply every payment in this file to the corresponding invoice, verify whether this carrier is eligible to take a shipment, or upload the proof of delivery and close the completed load. That statement gives the actions that follow a frame. The recording is no longer only a trace of what happened on the screen but instead becomes evidence about how a particular business outcome is achieved.

Multiple demonstrations can make that evidence richer. A single example shows how one case was handled, while several examples begin to reveal what persists and what varies. An exception shown deliberately might reveal more about the boundaries of the process than another repetition of the happy path. Two operators completing the same job differently may expose which actions are essential and which merely reflect individual habit.

Any remaining uncertainty can then be handled selectively. If Sola understands nearly all of a process but remains unsure why one record was excluded or whether a particular check should occur every time, it can ask about those moments rather than forcing the user to inspect the entire workflow. The user’s job begins to shift from building the automation toward correcting Sola’s understanding of the process.

FROM WORKFLOWS TO PRIMITIVES

A second opportunity appears as the number of automations inside one company grows. The first few workflows can reasonably be treated as independent objects. The hundredth makes that assumption more expensive because processes inside the same organization repeatedly rely on the same systems, rules, and operational capabilities.

Consider a logistics company where dozens of workflows interact with the same transportation management system. One might upload proof-of-delivery documents, another close completed loads, another retrieve shipment information, and another update billing records. Each could rely on some version of the same underlying actions, such as authenticating into the TMS, finding the relevant load, retrieving information, updating a record, and verifying that the change succeeded.

If every workflow independently implements those behaviors, automation creates duplication alongside leverage. A change to the TMS can require many workflows to rediscover the same fix, while every new automation has to rebuild capabilities the organization already possesses elsewhere.

The alternative is to let repeated behavior become a shared primitive. Authenticate to TMS, Find Load, Verify Carrier, or Post Payment can begin to look less like copied pieces of individual workflows and more like reusable capabilities available across the company.

This changes the economics of both creation and maintenance. A new automation can be assembled partly from capabilities the organization has already taught Sola, which shortens the path from demonstration to deployment. If the underlying system changes, the shared primitive could be repaired once, and the dependent workflows could inherit the improvement, provided the product can make those dependencies visible and test that the change preserves the intended behavior downstream.

The same logic can extend beyond mechanical interactions. A company may have a standard way to verify a carrier, interpret a particular document, reconcile a payment, approve an expense, or escalate an exception. Once represented cleanly enough, these could become part of a shared vocabulary from which future automations are composed.

Over time, Sola could begin to accumulate a reusable library of how the organization gets work done. Each new workflow could draw from that library while also contributing new capabilities back into it. The marginal automation becomes easier to build because more of the company’s operational knowledge already exists as software.

WHEN AUTOMATION BECOMES THE DEFAULT

As automation becomes easier, repetitive work can begin to acquire a natural home.

Today, those tasks are scattered across teams, systems, and individual habits. One person reconciles payments, another checks a portal, another updates a customer record, and another closes out a shipment. Sola creates the possibility of bringing that work into a common layer, where processes can be taught once, run repeatedly, and improved over time.

As more of an organization’s work moves through Sola, the product can become the natural starting point for the next process. Employees know where to turn when they encounter repetitive work, while the company gains a consistent place to capture how that work should happen and put it into production.

This is the larger opportunity. Sola can become the default environment for repetitive operational work: the place where companies encode it, run it, maintain it, and progressively hand more of it to software.

Over time, automation becomes less of a discrete initiative and more of the infrastructure through which the company operates.

The first recording may only be the beginning.
